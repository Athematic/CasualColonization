## 1. Country and the peoples in 1788

(Note: the care section, A6, A8.)

**The land was not empty.** When the First Fleet arrived in January 1788, the continent, Tasmania and the Torres Strait islands were the Country of hundreds of nations, each with its own language or dialect, law, land and history reaching back at least 50,000 years (65,000 by some dates; Mulvaney and Kamminga 1999; to be checked for the current consensus figure). "Country" in Aboriginal English means a people's land, waters and sky together, with the obligations that go with them; the game uses the word for a people's land and nothing more.

**How many nations and languages.** About 250 languages were spoken at the time of colonisation, with many more dialects; the AIATSIS map shows about 390 recorded language varieties and groupings (*National Indigenous Languages Report*, 2020, chapter 1). No single count of "nations" exists: the AIATSIS map shows the general locations of larger groupings, which may be language, social or nation groups, from published sources up to 1994 (David R. Horton, 1996, for the *Encyclopaedia of Aboriginal Australia*). AIATSIS says the map is not definitive and boundaries are not exact, and that it should not be used for native title or land claims. The game's simplified Country layer inherits all of these limits and adds its own (a hex is about 50 km across).

**How many people.** No accurate count is possible. The ABS gives a minimum of about 315,000 (Smith 1980) and notes estimates of over one million; Radcliffe-Brown (1930) proposed about 300,000; White and Mulvaney (1987) about 750,000; Butlin (1983) argued for much heavier early losses to smallpox than earlier estimates allowed. Sources: ABS, *Year Book Australia* (articles on the Aboriginal population); Butlin, *Our Original Aggression* (1983). The game does not need a number: peoples start with camps sized from their Country's food.

**Torres Strait Islanders** are a distinct people (or peoples), Melanesian in culture, living in villages on many of the strait's 274 islands, gardening, fishing and trading with Cape York and the Papuan coast. Two traditional languages: Meriam Mir (east) and Kala Lagaw Ya (west and central). Many Islanders use the name **Zenadth Kes** for the region, an acronym coined at a cultural workshop in 1989 (attributed by the Torres Strait Island Regional Council to the late Ephraim Bani; the meaning of its parts is given differently by different sources: to be checked, consult). Sources: Beckett, *Torres Strait Islanders* (1987); Torres Strait Island Regional Council, "Zenadth Kes acronym" (2025).

**Names.** The game uses each people's own name in the spelling communities use today, where a source shows it (Wiradjuri, Noongar, Yolŋu with ŋ, Palawa, Gunditjmara…). Alternative spellings are common (Kamilaroi or Gamilaraay, Darug or Dharug, Dharawal or Tharawal, Barkindji or Paakantyi): the table gives one and notes others. All spellings: consult.

## 3. Ways of life: seasons, fire, food, trade

(Note: A7, A9, A10.)

**Seasonal movement.** Most peoples moved within their Country through the year, from place to place as foods ripened and water was found, returning to known camping places; many also lived in more lasting houses at some places and seasons (Gunditjmara stone houses at Budj Bim; domed huts recorded in western Victoria and elsewhere). Many calendars have more than four seasons (six or more is common; the Bureau of Meteorology's *Indigenous Weather Knowledge* pages give examples from several peoples: to be checked). Sources: Mulvaney and Kamminga 1999; Gammage 2011; Bureau of Meteorology. The game's two seasons and two to four camping places are a simplification.

**Fire.** Many peoples burned their Country regularly, in cool, patchy fires, to keep grass for game, open the land for travel, encourage plants and reduce the fuel for great fires. The archaeologist Rhys Jones called it "fire-stick farming" (1969). Gammage (2011) argues that burning shaped much of the continent into a managed estate; Steffensen (2020) writes from current practice. The practice has cultural and spiritual dimensions the game does not model. After colonisation, where burning stopped, fuel built up and some country changed; colonists' own fires and grazing changed it too (research section 9). Sources: Jones 1969; Gammage 2011; Steffensen 2020.

**Food.** Hunting (kangaroo, wallaby, possum, emu, birds), fishing (with nets, spears, hooks, traps and weirs), shellfish, and plant foods: seeds of grasses and other plants ground on grindstones into flour; yams, murnong (yam daisy) and other tubers; fruit. **Fish and eel traps:** stone traps and channels such as Budj Bim's (World Heritage, 2019, with eel channels dated to about 6,600 years ago: to be checked) and the Brewarrina fish traps (Baiame's Ngunnu) on the Barwon. Sources: UNESCO 2019; Mulvaney and Kamminga 1999.

**Farming or hunting and gathering?** How far these practices amount to agriculture is debated. Bruce Pascoe's *Dark Emu* (2014) argued for farming and villages; Sutton and Walshe (2021) answered that the record shows skilled hunter-gatherers with deep land management, not farmers. The game models what both sides agree was done (burning, traps, seed grinding, tuber harvest, seasonal camps, some lasting houses), and makes no claim either way.

**Torres Strait Islanders** gardened (yams, taro, sweet potato, bananas, coconuts), fished, hunted turtle and dugong, and sailed outrigger canoes, some of them obtained from Papua in exchange. Source: Beckett 1987.

**Trade networks.** Goods travelled far by exchange between neighbours and at gatherings: greenstone axes from the Mount William quarry (Wurundjeri Country) across south-eastern Australia (McBryde 1978, 1984); ochre from mines such as Wilgie Mia (WA) and Parachilna (Flinders Ranges); shells from the north coast deep into the interior; pituri (a stimulant plant) from the Mulligan River; possum-skin cloaks in the south-east. Sources: McBryde 1978; Mulvaney and Kamminga 1999; pituri and ochre routes: to be sourced. The game uses ochre, stone axes, possum-skin cloaks and shells as goods in barter between peoples (A10); pituri is left out.

**The Macassans.** From at least the 1700s (some evidence earlier) until 1907, fishing fleets from Makassar in Sulawesi sailed each wet season to the Arnhem Land coast (Marege') to gather and process trepang (sea cucumber) for the China trade, trading cloth, metal tools, rice, tobacco and dugout canoes with the Yolŋu and others; they did not settle. After Federation the government stopped the trade; the last fleet left in 1907. Sources: Macknight 1976; National Museum of Australia, "Trade with the Makasar" (Defining Moments).

**Not modelled:** the Dreaming, songlines, ceremonies, initiation, kinship systems and moieties, sacred sites, totems. These are central to the peoples' lives and law, and much of the knowledge is restricted. The game shows their absence as a choice, not as a gap in the peoples.

## 4. Contact, the frontier and survival

(Note: the care section, A6, A15, A18, A20.)

- **Smallpox** struck the peoples of the Sydney harbour in 1789, killing very many; its origin is debated (Butlin 1983; Campbell, *Invisible Invaders*, 2002; to be checked). A second epidemic spread in the south-east about 1829–31. Measles, influenza and other diseases followed. In the game, epidemics are Beta 3's mechanic (the same rules for both sides, Simon's decision), not events.
- **Frontier wars.** Peoples resisted the taking of their Country across the continent from 1788 to the 1920s and later: the Hawkesbury and Nepean wars (1790s–1816), the Bathurst war with the Wiradjuri (1822–24), the war in Van Diemen's Land (1820s–1832), the Swan River (1830s), Port Phillip and Gippsland (1840s), Queensland (1840s–1880s), the Kimberley (1890s). Sources: Reynolds 1981 and 2013; Connor, *The Australian Frontier Wars, 1788–1838* (2002).
- **Massacres.** The University of Newcastle's *Colonial Frontier Massacres in Australia, 1788–1930* (led by Lyndall Ryan, d. 2024) mapped at least 414 massacres (six or more undefended people killed in one operation) with more than 10,000 Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander victims; the data is archived at the Australian Data Archive. The Myall Creek massacre (1838) was the first after which colonists were hanged for killing Aboriginal people. The game does not model any of this as an action or event (design note, care section) and its scenario text says that it happened.
- **"Terra nullius"** names the doctrine, applied in practice from 1788, that the land belonged to no one the Crown had to treat with. In 1835 John Batman's "treaty" with Kulin elders for land at Port Phillip was declared void by Governor Bourke's proclamation (26 August 1835: to be checked), which asserted the Crown's ownership. The High Court rejected the doctrine in *Mabo v Queensland (No 2)* (1992). Sources: Attwood, *Possession* (2009); Reynolds, *The Law of the Land* (1987).
- **Petitions and protest.** Peoples petitioned the Crown and colonial governments: the Palawa at Wybalenna petitioned Queen Victoria in 1846; the Kulin at Coranderrk, with William Barak, petitioned and walked to Melbourne in the 1870s–80s to keep their land. Sources: Ryan 2012; Barwick 1998. The game's `recognition` way (A18) draws on these: the treaty that was asked for and not made.
- **Protection, missions and the Stolen Generations.** Laws to control Aboriginal people's lives began in the colonies (Victoria's Aboriginal Protection Act of 1869; to be checked for others), and the removal of children became systematic mostly after 1901 (*Bringing Them Home*, Human Rights and Equal Opportunity Commission, 1997). **Not modelled** in any form.
- **The Native Police** (Port Phillip 1842, New South Wales and Queensland later) were Aboriginal troopers under colonial officers, used in many killings. **Not modelled.**
- **Survival.** Peoples survived, kept or returned to Country, worked in the pastoral industry (often unpaid), on the goldfields (Cahir 2012), and kept their languages and law; the Yolŋu and many desert and northern peoples held most of their Country through 1901.

**The French.** Jean-François de La Pérouse anchored in Botany Bay days after the First Fleet (January 1788); d'Entrecasteaux charted the south (1792–93); Nicolas Baudin's expedition (1800–04) met Matthew Flinders at Encounter Bay (1802), and its maps named the south coast "Terre Napoléon"; the British settled the Derwent in 1803 partly to forestall France (to be checked: how far this was the motive). In 1772 Louis de Saint Aloüarn claimed western Australia for France at Shark Bay. Sources: Horner, *The French Reconnaissance* (1987, to be checked); Fornasiero, Monteath and West-Sooby, *Encountering Terra Australis* (2004).

**The Dutch.** Dutch ships charted the west and north coasts from 1606 (the *Duyfken*) through the 17th century ("New Holland"), from the VOC's base at Batavia; no settlement was made. Portugal held Timor. Both are alternative history only.

**Companies.** The Australian Agricultural Company (1824; a million acres at Port Stephens, on Worimi Country), the Van Diemen's Land Company (1825; the north-west of Tasmania), the South Australian Company (1835–36). Sources: ADB and company histories; to be sourced.

**The route.** The First Fleet sailed from Portsmouth on 13 May 1787, by Tenerife, Rio de Janeiro and the Cape of Good Hope, and reached Botany Bay on 18–20 January 1788: about eight months. Clippers on the great circle route in the 1850s took about three months; steamers and the Suez Canal (1869) shortened it further. Blainey, *The Tyranny of Distance* (1966), is the classic account of distance in Australian history.

## 7. Convicts

(Note: A11.)

- **Numbers:** about 162,000 convicts were transported to the Australian colonies between 1788 and 1868, on about 600 voyages (figures range from about 159,000 to 165,000); the last ship, the *Hougoumont*, reached Fremantle in January 1868. Western Australia received about 9,600–10,000. Sources: National Museum of Australia, "Convict transportation ends" (Defining Moments); Hirst 1983.
- **End of transportation:** New South Wales 1840 (one ship in 1849 met protest); Van Diemen's Land 1853; Moreton Bay's penal settlement closed 1842; Western Australia 1850–1868 (at the colonists' request, for labour). The Anti-Transportation League (1849–53) campaigned across the eastern colonies. Victoria and South Australia received none as such (Victoria received some "exiles" in the 1840s: to be checked).
- **Assignment:** convicts worked for the government or were assigned to free settlers.
- **Ticket of leave:** permission to work for oneself within a district before the sentence ended. Governor Brisbane's regulations (1821, by most sources; the Museums of History NSW guide credits Macquarie with minimum periods) set minimum service before a ticket: about four years for a seven-year sentence, six to eight for fourteen, ten to twelve for life (some sources give eight for life). Then a **certificate of freedom** at the sentence's end, or a **pardon** (conditional or absolute). Sources: Museums of History NSW, guides to tickets of leave and pardons; Biographical Database of Australia, "Tickets of leave". To be checked: the exact rules by year.
- **Emancipists:** freed convicts and their place in colonial society; Macquarie appointed emancipists to office (Greenway, Redfern). Hirst 1983.
- **The Americas:** under the Transportation Act of 1718 about 50,000 convicts were sent to the American colonies, mostly Maryland and Virginia, until the War of Independence; they were sold into service for their terms (seven or fourteen years). Source: Ekirch, *Bound for America* (1987).

## 8. The economy: wool, wheat, the sea, gold

(Note: A12, A13, A14.)

- **Wool.** Spanish merino sheep reached New South Wales from the Cape in 1797; John and Elizabeth Macarthur bred them at Camden and Elizabeth Farm (Elizabeth ran the farm during John's long absences). Wool became the colonies' main export from the 1830s. Sources: ADB, "Macarthur, Elizabeth", "Macarthur, John".
- **Squatting:** from the 1820s graziers took up "runs" beyond the official limits of settlement (the Nineteen Counties, 1829); licences from 1836. Runs covered vast areas of Country, and much of the frontier violence followed the flocks. Sources: Roberts, *The Squatting Age in Australia, 1835–1847* (1935); Reynolds 1981.
- **Wheat:** South Australia became the main wheat colony from the 1840s; Goyder's Line (1865) marked where rainfall was too unreliable for crops. Source: to be sourced (SA State Library).
- **Timber:** red cedar cut along the coastal rivers from the 1790s.
- **Whaling and sealing:** sealing in Bass Strait from 1798; bay whaling at Hobart and along the southern coast from the 1800s–1840s; seals and whales were hunted down within decades. Sealers abducted Palawa women to Bass Strait islands; their descendants are part of today's Tasmanian Aboriginal community (Ryan 2012). The abductions are not modelled.
- **Copper:** Kapunda (1844) and Burra (1845) in South Australia.
- **Gold:** Edward Hargraves announced gold near Bathurst (Ophir) in 1851; Ballarat and Bendigo in Victoria later that year; the rushes brought hundreds of thousands of immigrants in the 1850s, from Britain, Ireland, Europe, America and China. Later fields: Gympie (1867), Charters Towers (1871), the Palmer (1873), Coolgardie (1892), Kalgoorlie (1893). Aboriginal people worked and guided on the Victorian goldfields (Cahir 2012). Chinese miners met riots (Buckland 1857, Lambing Flat 1860–61) and discriminatory laws; this history is in the research, not the rules. Sources: Serle, *The Golden Age* (1963); National Museum of Australia, "Gold rushes" (Defining Moments); Cahir 2012.
- **The miners' licence and Eureka:** Victoria's licence fee and its enforcement led to the Eureka Stockade at Ballarat, 3 December 1854, where troops and police attacked the diggers' stockade (about 22 diggers and 6 soldiers killed: to be checked); the licence was replaced by the miner's right and diggers won the vote. Sources: ADB, "Lalor, Peter"; National Museum of Australia, "Eureka Stockade".
- **Sugar and Pacific Islander labour:** Queensland's sugar industry from the 1860s used tens of thousands of South Sea Islanders, many recruited by deception or force ("blackbirding"); they were largely deported after 1901. **Not modelled:** sugar is grown by colonists in the game; consult the Australian South Sea Islander community if sugar's story is told in text.
- **Silver:** Broken Hill, 1883.

## 9. Water, drought and fire

(Note: A9, A20.)

- **Droughts:** severe droughts struck in, among others, 1838–39, 1864–66, the 1880s and the **Federation Drought** (about 1895–1903), the worst of the century in the east, which killed tens of millions of sheep. Sources: Bureau of Meteorology, drought history pages (to be checked); Garden, *Droughts, Floods and Cyclones* (2009).
- **Water works:** tanks and dams on stations; artesian bores into the Great Artesian Basin from the late 1870s–80s (first flowing bore: to be checked). Paddle steamers on the Murray from 1853 (Randell, Cadell).
- **Bushfires:** Black Thursday, 6 February 1851, burned about a quarter of Victoria (to be checked: the area); Red Tuesday, 1 February 1898, in South Gippsland. Source: Griffiths, *Forests of Ash* (2001).
- **Fire and the end of burning:** where Aboriginal burning stopped, fuel built up in some country and woodlands thickened; colonists also burned and grazed. The relation of fire regimes to colonisation is argued over; the game's simple rule (less burning, worse fires) follows Gammage 2011 and Steffensen 2020 and is to be checked against fire-history research (to be sourced).
- **Rabbits** from 1859 (Barwon Park, Victoria) spread across the south-east and degraded pastures.

## 10. Exploration

(Note: A20.)

Aboriginal guides and the peoples whose Country they crossed were part of almost every expedition. Dates to be checked.
- The crossing of the Blue Mountains: Blaxland, Lawson and Wentworth, May–June 1813.
- Hume and Hovell to Port Phillip, 1824–25.
- Charles Sturt down the Murrumbidgee and Murray to the sea, 1829–30.
- Thomas Mitchell's expeditions, 1830s–40s; Yuranigh (Wiradjuri) guided the 1845–46 expedition (ADB, "Yuranigh").
- Edward John Eyre across the Bight to Albany, 1840–41, with Wylie (Noongar) (ADB, "Wylie").
- Ludwig Leichhardt to Port Essington, 1844–45; lost in 1848.
- Edmund Kennedy, killed 1848; Galmahra (Jackey Jackey) survived and led the rescue (ADB, "Jackey Jackey").
- Burke and Wills, 1860–61: died at Cooper Creek; John King survived with the Yandruwandha.
- John McDouall Stuart crossed south to north, 1862; the Overland Telegraph followed his route (1872).

## 11. Self-government and Federation

(Note: A15, A16, A17.)

- **Responsible government:** the Australian Colonies Government Act (1850) and the colonies' own constitutions brought responsible government to New South Wales, Victoria, Tasmania and South Australia in 1855–56, Queensland at its separation (1859) and Western Australia in 1890. South Australia gave women the vote in 1894 (and the right to stand). Sources: Hirst, *The Strange Birth of Colonial Democracy* (1988, to be checked).
- **Federation:** the Federal Council of Australasia (1885; New South Wales and South Australia stayed out at first); Parkes's Tenterfield Oration (24 October 1889); the Australasian Federation Conference, Melbourne, 1890; the National Australasian Convention, Sydney, 1891 (a draft constitution, largely Griffith's and Inglis Clark's); the Corowa Conference (1893) and John Quick's plan for elected conventions and referendums; the Federal Convention, 1897–98; referendums in 1898 (New South Wales short of its required yes vote), 1899 (New South Wales, Victoria, South Australia, Tasmania, Queensland), and Western Australia on 31 July 1900; the Commonwealth of Australia Constitution Act (assent 9 July 1900); proclamation 1 January 1901. Protection (Victoria) against free trade (New South Wales) was a main dispute. Sources: Hirst, *The Sentimental Nation* (2000); Irving, *To Constitute a Nation* (1997); National Museum of Australia, "Federation".
- **The Constitution and Aboriginal people:** sections 51(xxvi) and 127 of the 1901 Constitution excluded Aboriginal people from the Commonwealth's race power and from population counts, until the 1967 referendum. Context only.
