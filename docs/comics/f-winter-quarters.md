# Winter Quarters

**Logline.** In the first winter of a tiny river colony, the settlers count their barrels and find forty days of food for a ninety-day winter. When a party of Wendat traders arrives across the ice with a loaded toboggan, they bring corn and fish, but also a sharper eye for weather, smoke and snow than the newcomers have. When a blizzard catches three children out on the frozen river, the two households have to work as one to bring them home.

**Cover moment.** The Lebrun family greets the Wendat visitors in the snow outside the palisade while Yandeia kneels to tie a snowshoe for young Jacquot, the toboggan of corn, smoked fish and furs beside them, Gilles splitting wood and old Rémi carrying a bucket from the ice hole behind. It appears on **page 4**.

**Setting.** Sainte-Aube, a new settlement of six log cabins inside a palisade, on a clearing above a frozen river north of the St. Lawrence. January 1699.

**Cast.**
- **Mathurin Lebrun**: carpenter, and the colony's leader because nobody else wanted the job. Big, bearded, brown fur hat, long dark-brown coat with a fur collar. Kind, stubborn, hopeless on snow.
- **Perrine Lebrun**: his wife, keeper of the stores. White linen cap, blue coat trimmed with fur, red scarf. Counts everything in chalk on the storehouse door.
- **Aimée Lebrun** (9): red coat with fur trim and a fur hat. Bold, loud, the first to see everything.
- **Jacquot Lebrun** (11): brown coat with fur cuffs, fur hat. A dreamer who wants to be a great hunter and keeps sinking to his waist.
- **Gilles Fauchon**: settler and woodsplitter. Brown coat, round felt hat, short beard. A grumbler with a soft heart.
- **Old Rémi**: Breton fisherman. Blue coat, red knitted cap, carries the water bucket. Very proud of knowing about fish.
- **Ondaha**: Wendat trader. Red-and-white woven headband with one feather, long tan coat with red-and-blue trim, braids. Calm, dry, misses nothing.
- **Yandeia**: Ondaha's sister, a maker of snowshoes. Tan coat with red trim, two long braids, red skirt. Patient, very funny without smiling.
- **Tsiwa** (14): Ondaha's daughter. Braids, tan coat with a fringed, patterned sash. Fast on snowshoes, laughs easily, becomes Aimée's friend.

*(The Wendat personal names are invented for this story. Confirm spellings and suitability with a Wendat language consultant before lettering. "Kwe" is the Wendat greeting.)*

## PAGE 1

1. Wide establishing panel, almost half the page. A white forest clearing seen from a low hill: six snow-loaded log cabins inside a palisade of sharpened logs, a frozen river curving away to blue-white hills. Only three of the six chimneys are smoking.
   CAPTION: January, 1699. The river froze in November.
   CAPTION: The supply ship from Quebec was to come before the ice. It did not come.

2. Medium shot, on the river ice below the palisade. Old Rémi sits on an upturned bucket beside a square hole in the ice, a short fishing line in his mittens, looking offended.
   RÉMI: Forty years I fished off Brittany. Breton fish know me.
   RÉMI: These fish are very rude.

3. Interior of the storehouse, lit by one candle. Barrels, two sacks, a half-empty crate of dried peas. Perrine in her white cap stands at the door, chalk in hand, beside rows of chalk tally marks. Mathurin holds the candle up.
   PERRINE: Peas, flour, salt pork, turnips. I have counted it three times.
   MATHURIN: And?

4. Close on Perrine's face, very calm, which is worse than if she shouted.
   PERRINE: Forty days, Mathurin. Forty days of food.

5. Close on Mathurin, candlelight under his beard.
   MATHURIN: And the winter?
   PERRINE (off): Ninety days. If it is kind.

6. Small panel. Outside the storehouse door, Jacquot crouches in the snow with his ear to the planks, eyes wide.
   JACQUOT (small, to himself): Then somebody has to go hunting.

## PAGE 2

1. Morning. Jacquot slips out of the palisade gate with a coil of snare cord and a sack, his chin set like a hero's. The snow beyond the gate is smooth and deep.
   JACQUOT: A great hunter needs no breakfast.

2. Same framing, two steps later. Jacquot has sunk to his waist. Only his top half shows, still heroic.
   SFX: FWUMP

3. Aimée, in her red coat, stands on the packed path behind him, hands on hips.
   AIMÉE: Great hunters are taller.
   JACQUOT: Go home, Aimée.
   AIMÉE: You go home. I'll watch.

4. Jacquot has wallowed forward to the edge of the trees. He freezes, staring down. Aimée has followed in his trench. In the snow before them: a line of huge, oval prints, each with a criss-cross pattern pressed into it.
   AIMÉE: Jacquot. Those are giant's feet.
   JACQUOT: ...Giants with baskets on their feet?

5. Mathurin wades up behind them, sinking to his knees with every step, red in the face from the effort and from worry. He grabs each child by a collar.
   MATHURIN: Home. Both of you. Now.

6. The three of them look back at the oval prints, which skim across the deep snow without sinking at all.
   MATHURIN: If giants walk here, they walk better than we do.

## PAGE 3

1. Next morning. Aimée is up on the walkway inside the palisade, pointing out across the river ice, shouting.
   AIMÉE: PAPA! People on the river! With a sled!

2. Long shot from the palisade: three figures on snowshoes come up the white river, small and dark, pulling a long wooden toboggan heaped with bundles.
   CAPTION: They came the way the giants' tracks had gone.

3. Inside the gate. Gilles has snatched up his splitting axe and holds it across his chest, nervous. Mathurin puts a big hand on the axe handle and lowers it.
   GILLES: Who are they? What do they want?
   MATHURIN: Put that down, Gilles. Nobody arrives with a full sled to start a quarrel.

4. Outside the gate. The three Wendat visitors stop at a polite distance. Ondaha, in his woven headband with its single feather, raises an open hand. Yandeia and Tsiwa stand beside the toboggan.
   ONDAHA: Kwe!
   MATHURIN (raising his own hand, uncertain): Kwe... Good day!

5. Close on Ondaha, a slight, knowing smile. He nods towards the cabins behind Mathurin.
   ONDAHA: We saw your smoke from the bend of the river. Six houses. In the autumn, six fires.
   ONDAHA: Now, three. A house that saves its wood is a worried house.

6. Mathurin, caught out, rubs the back of his neck. Perrine has come up beside him and lifts an eyebrow at her husband.
   PERRINE: He reads chimneys.
   MATHURIN: Apparently, so does everybody except me.

## PAGE 4

*(Cover moment, as one large panel taking the top two-thirds of the page, followed by two small panels.)*

1. LARGE PANEL, matching the cover. Snowy foreground outside the palisade. The Lebrun family stands together at left: Mathurin in his fur hat with a hand on Aimée's shoulder, Perrine in blue leaning down to watch, Jacquot standing with one foot forward. Yandeia kneels before Jacquot, tying the rawhide bindings of a snowshoe to his boot. Ondaha and Tsiwa stand at right beside the toboggan, piled with dried corn, smoked fish and bundled furs and tied down with rope. Behind them Gilles has gone back to splitting logs on a stump, and Rémi carries a bucket away from the hole in the ice. Snow-heavy conifers frame both sides; blue hills beyond; smoke rising straight up.
   YANDEIA: First the toes. Then around the heel. Tight where it matters, loose where you must move.
   YANDEIA: A snowshoe is like a good friend.

2. Close on Jacquot looking down at his foot, utterly entranced.
   JACQUOT: You made these?
   YANDEIA: Ash wood for the frame. Babiche (rawhide strip) for the web. And all of last winter for the patience.

3. Tsiwa leans towards Aimée, both girls eyeing each other with frank curiosity.
   TSIWA: Your coat is the colour of a cardinal bird.
   AIMÉE: Your sled is the size of a boat!
   TSIWA: It is a boat. A boat for snow.

## PAGE 5

1. Interior of the Lebrun cabin. Everyone is crowded round the hearth: settlers on benches, the Wendat visitors seated on furs near the fire. A heap of dried corn cobs and a row of smoked fish lie on the table between them.
   CAPTION: Corn from the Wendat fields, braided and dried in the autumn. Whitefish, smoked over maple.

2. Perrine, very businesslike, slides her most precious object across the table: a polished copper kettle.
   PERRINE: I know the price of corn in Quebec. This was my mother's. It is worth a great deal.

3. Ondaha looks at the kettle, then at Perrine, and laughs, not unkindly. Yandeia hides a smile behind her hand.
   ONDAHA: Keep your mother's kettle. Food in the hungry season is not sold among neighbours.
   ONDAHA: But trade is still trade. We do want something.

4. Ondaha points through the open door to Mathurin's sawpit and the big two-man saw hanging on the cabin wall.
   ONDAHA: The snow came heavy at our town. A great roof beam of our longhouse has cracked. Our people can cut it, but your saw can cut it straight, and quickly.
   ONDAHA: Your saw, and the man who knows it.

5. Mathurin stands, delighted to be useful at last, and lifts the saw off its pegs.
   MATHURIN: Now that I understand! A beam, I can do!
   PERRINE (dry): Sit down. It is still dark in three hours.

6. Ondaha, half serious, half teasing, taps the table.
   ONDAHA: One more thing. Your palisade has its gate towards the forest.
   ONDAHA: Friends come by the river. Next year, cut a door that faces the water, and we will bring you furs and corn each season.
   GILLES (aside to Rémi): A trading post. In our own fence.

## PAGE 6

1. Next day on the river ice. Yandeia and Tsiwa feed a long net under the ice from one hole to another with a pole and a line. Rémi watches, arms folded, sceptical.
   RÉMI: A net under the ice? Fish do not go into nets in winter. They sulk on the bottom.

2. Tsiwa, grinning, hauls up the net. It is full of flapping silver whitefish. Rémi's mouth falls open.
   SFX: FLAP FLAP FLAP

3. Rémi, overcome, kneels and pats a fish on its head as though it were a long-lost cousin.
   RÉMI: Oh, you beauty. You were just waiting for better manners.
   TSIWA: You were fishing at the wrong place. Whitefish travel along the edge of the deep water. Like people on a road.

4. On the bank. Jacquot, on his own snowshoes now, takes a confident stride forward, steps on his own other snowshoe, and pitches face first into the snow. Aimée and Tsiwa double over laughing.
   SFX: FLUMPF

5. Yandeia hauls Jacquot up by the back of his collar, his face plastered white.
   YANDEIA: Wide steps. You are walking like a man in a narrow church. The forest is wide.
   JACQUOT (spitting snow): Wide. Wide. Yes.

6. Small panel. Gilles, at the woodpile, has been watching too. He secretly tries a "wide step" in the deep snow without snowshoes and sinks to his hips. He looks around to make sure nobody saw.

## PAGE 7

1. Late afternoon. Ondaha stands on the palisade walk, shading his eyes and looking at a low winter sun. On either side of the sun are two small bright spots of light, with a faint ring around it (sun dogs).
   ONDAHA: Look. The sun is wearing two lamps tonight.

2. Mathurin squints beside him.
   MATHURIN: Very pretty.
   ONDAHA: Pretty today. Tomorrow, a storm. Perhaps tonight. We will stay with you and go to our town when it has passed.

3. Down below on the river, small in the panel, three figures head out to the net holes with a little toboggan: Tsiwa leading on snowshoes, Jacquot on his snowshoes, Aimée trotting in their tracks.
   AIMÉE (small): Last check of the net! Then soup!

4. Perrine at the cabin door, wiping her hands, calls after them. Behind her, the sky to the north has turned a flat, heavy grey.
   PERRINE: Straight there and straight back!
   JACQUOT (tiny, far off): YES, MAMAN!

5. Close on the edge of the forest across the river. A wall of white is moving through the trees, bending them.
   SFX: WHOOOOO

## PAGE 8

1. Full-width panel. Out on the river ice, the blizzard strikes: everything turns white, the three children are suddenly small grey shapes, the bank gone, the palisade gone.
   SFX: WHHAAAOOOOO

2. Close on the three, huddled together beside the little toboggan, hoods pulled tight. Aimée clutches Tsiwa's sash.
   AIMÉE: Which way is home? Which way is ANYTHING?
   JACQUOT: This way! I'm sure! ...No. That way.

3. Tsiwa grabs Jacquot's arm firmly as he starts off.
   TSIWA: Stop. Don't walk. My grandmother says a lost person walks in a circle until the circle eats him.

4. Tsiwa turns the toboggan on its side against the wind and pulls the other two down behind it into its shelter.
   TSIWA: We make a wall. We wait. We listen. Our people will come.
   AIMÉE (very small): How will they find us in all this?

5. Close on Jacquot, frightened, but thinking hard. He looks down at the snowshoes on his feet.
   JACQUOT (thought): Tight where it matters...

## PAGE 9

1. In the colony. The cabin door bangs open in the wind. Perrine stands in the gale, shouting into white nothing. Mathurin is already pulling on his coat.
   PERRINE: JACQUOT! AIMÉE!
   MATHURIN: I'm going out.

2. Ondaha stands in the doorway and blocks him, calm and immovable.
   ONDAHA: No. If you go without snowshoes, we will be looking for four people instead of three.
   ONDAHA: We do this properly.

3. Inside, quick action. Yandeia uncoils the toboggan rope; Gilles brings every rope from the storehouse; they knot them end to end.
   YANDEIA: A line from your gate to the river. Whoever walks out holds it. Whoever is lost comes to it.

4. Rémi, struck by an idea, snatches up Perrine's copper kettle and Gilles's axe and looks at Perrine for permission. She nods fiercely.
   RÉMI: In Brittany, in fog, we ring the church bell for the boats.
   RÉMI: We have no bell. We have your mother.

5. At the palisade gate, Gilles strikes the kettle with the back of the axe, again and again.
   SFX: BONNNG... BONNNG... BONNNG...

6. Ondaha, on his snowshoes, ties the rope line round his waist and steps into the white. Mathurin pays out the rope behind him, jaw tight.
   ONDAHA: Keep ringing. Do not stop, even if your arms fall off.
   GILLES: They won't. I've split wood all winter for this.

## PAGE 10

1. Behind the toboggan wall. The three children raise their heads at once.
   SFX (faint): ...bonnng...
   AIMÉE: Maman's kettle!

2. Tsiwa holds up a hand, listening, turning her head slowly.
   TSIWA: The wind bends the sound. Listen to where it is loudest, not where it seems to come from.
   TSIWA: There. Up the river and to the left.

3. Problem: Aimée has no snowshoes. She steps forward and sinks to her thighs. Jacquot looks at the little toboggan, then at her.
   JACQUOT: Get in the boat for snow.
   AIMÉE: You can't pull me. You can't even walk.

4. Jacquot, leaning into the rope of the toboggan, takes a wide, slow, perfect snowshoe step, then another, Aimée sitting in the toboggan behind him, Tsiwa breaking trail in front.
   JACQUOT: Wide steps. The forest is wide.
   SFX (louder): BONNNG

5. Out of the white ahead looms Ondaha, the rope line trailing from his waist. Tsiwa runs into his arms.
   ONDAHA: There you are. You stopped and waited?
   TSIWA: Like Grandmother says.
   ONDAHA: Good. She will be unbearable when she hears.

6. Ondaha puts the rope in Jacquot's mittened hands and looks at him with new respect.
   ONDAHA: You pulled your sister this far on your own feet? Then you can pull her home along the rope.

## PAGE 11

1. Inside the Lebrun cabin, that night. Everyone from the colony and all three Wendat visitors are packed into one room around one big fire. Blankets, furs, bowls of corn soup with fish. Outside, the wind still howls.
   CAPTION: That night, all of Sainte-Aube slept in one cabin around one fire.

2. Yandeia, ladling soup, explains to Perrine, who nods slowly.
   YANDEIA: In our longhouses, many families share the fires down the middle. One fire warms many.
   PERRINE: Six cabins, six fires... We have been heating six empty rooms.

3. Ondaha tells a story to the children with his hands making shapes in the firelight; Aimée and Tsiwa lie side by side on a fur, listening. Yandeia, behind him, glares.
   ONDAHA: ...and so, on her very first snowshoes, my sister walked straight across the beaver pond, straight onto the beavers' house, and straight through the roof.
   AIMÉE: What did the beavers do?
   ONDAHA: They moved. They said the neighbourhood had gone down.

4. Rémi, not to be outdone, stands up with a fish in one hand to tell his own story. Gilles groans and pulls his hat over his eyes.
   RÉMI: Now in BRITTANY there was a herring as big as a church—
   GILLES: Not the herring again.

5. Perrine, at the table with her chalk and a board, redoes her sums with the corn and fish counted in. Mathurin looks over her shoulder.
   PERRINE: Corn from our neighbours. Fish from the net. Wood for one fire instead of six...
   PERRINE: Ninety days. Perhaps a hundred.
   MATHURIN: Then winter can be as long as it likes.

6. Small panel. Jacquot, asleep sitting up, still has one snowshoe on.

## PAGE 12

1. Morning. Bright, clear, still. Smoke rises dead straight from one chimney into a pale blue sky. The snow is new and smooth.
   CAPTION: The storm blew itself out by dawn.

2. At the river gate, Gilles is already sawing through the palisade, making a new opening that faces the river. A small sign-board leans against the fence beside him, blank, waiting to be painted.
   GILLES: A door towards the water. So friends know which way to come.

3. The departure. Ondaha and Yandeia pack the toboggan with Mathurin's big two-man saw and his tool bag. Perrine presses the copper kettle into Ondaha's hands.
   PERRINE: Not as a price. As a bell. For your town, when the snow comes.
   ONDAHA (accepting it with both hands): Then it will ring for both our houses.

4. Tsiwa and Aimée press their foreheads together in goodbye. Tsiwa ties a small strip of her patterned sash round Aimée's wrist.
   TSIWA: When the ice goes out, we come by canoe. You still owe me a cardinal-red mitten.
   AIMÉE: I'll knit you two!

5. Mathurin stands proudly in a brand-new pair of snowshoes that Yandeia has made for him, saw on his back, ready to set off with the Wendat party for their town.
   YANDEIA: Your own pair. Made extra wide.
   MATHURIN: For a man in a narrow church?
   YANDEIA: For a man with big feet.

6. FINAL PANEL, wide. Mathurin's first great step out of the gate has ended with him flat on his face in the snow, fur hat rolling away. Everyone, settlers and Wendat alike, is laughing. Jacquot, walking easily on his own snowshoes, holds out a hand to his father. Behind them, the single straight column of smoke, the new river gate, and the white river leading away towards the Wendat town.
   JACQUOT: First the toes, Papa.
