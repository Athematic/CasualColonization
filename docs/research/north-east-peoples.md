## 1. The peoples

(Note: T20, section 13 "The peoples".)

**Many nations, many ways.** The peoples of the North-east were many nations with different ways: the farming towns of the Haudenosaunee and the Wendat beside the hunting and fishing peoples of the coast and the north, such as the Mi'kmaq, the Innu and the Beothuk. This is why every number in the game (what a people grows and stores, how hard it hunts, which ways of winning it values) is data per people and per scenario, not one outlook for all.
Source: Bruce G. Trigger (ed.), *Handbook of North American Indians, vol. 15: Northeast* (Smithsonian Institution, 1978).

**Around the year 1000** (T20's research brief): the ancestors of the Beothuk (archaeologists' "Little Passage" culture), the Dorset (an archaeologists' name; Inuit tradition remembers the Tuniit), the ancestors of the Innu and the Mi'kmaq; the Thule (ancestors of the Inuit) reaching Labrador later; the St. Lawrence Iroquoians (met by Cartier, gone by 1600) and the Haudenosaunee. Some of these peoples lived a seasonal round, which the game leaves to the later `seasonal` lifeway (Australia's work).

Nineteen peoples, north to south. Names are the peoples' own where well established, with the common English name in the note; archaeological names are marked. Homelands are those the sources give for the 16th and early 17th centuries, the earliest that written sources describe.

**Peoples who come and go.** The Dorset are no longer found on northern Labrador's and Nunavik's coasts after about 1350; the Inuit come to them about 1400; the St. Lawrence Iroquoians are no longer found after about 1580. The years lie within the ranges the sources give. The gap of fifty years between the Dorset and the Inuit avoids saying that one replaced the other, which the sources do not settle. Nothing names a cause.

**The Haudenosaunee and the "Iroquois".** "Haudenosaunee", "people of the longhouse", is the Five Nations' own name; "Iroquois" is the name the French gave. The North-east uses the people's own name.

**Each people's own values** (a first reading of the sources, behind the per-people score weights and stores in the note's section 13):
- **The Haudenosaunee** weigh their council and their treaties: the League and its council (Snow 1994; Richter 1992).
- **The Wendat** weigh trust and treaties, and kept a larger store: the corn they grew to trade north (Trigger 1976).
- **The Mi'kmaq and the Innu** weigh the land's health and their homeland; the Innu stored food less (Prins 1996; Helm (ed.) 1981).
- No temperaments are given: the sources give none, and naming a people's temper risks a stereotype.

**Town names** for the playable peoples' later towns (places the sources record):
- **Haudenosaunee:** towns of the Five Nations recorded in the 17th and 18th centuries: Onondaga, Ganondagan, Kanonwalohale, Tiononderoge, Canajoharie, Kanatsiohareke, Goiogouen, Tionontoguen, Ossernenon, Totiakton, Oneida Castle, Chenussio.
- **Wendat:** Wendat towns of the 1620s–1640s, as the Jesuits recorded them: Ossossané, Teanaostaiaé, Taenhatentaron, Cahiagué, Ihonatiria, Scanonaenrat, Contarea, Toanché, Arontaen.
- **Mi'kmaq:** places of Mi'kma'ki by their Mi'kmaw names, in use today: Kjipuktuk (Halifax), Sipekne'katik (the Shubenacadie), Piktuk (Pictou), Elsipogtog (Richibucto), Listuguj (Restigouche), Esgenoôpetitj (Burnt Church), Unama'ki (Cape Breton), Epekwitk (Prince Edward Island), Wagmatcook, Eskasoni.
- **Innu:** Innu communities of Nitassinan by their Innu names: Uashat (Sept-Îles), Ekuanitshit (Mingan), Nutashkuan (Natashquan), Sheshatshiu, Mashteuiatsh, Pessamit (Betsiamites), Unamen Shipu (La Romaine), Pakua Shipi (Saint-Augustin), Matimekush (Schefferville), Natuashish.
- Using today's community names for towns of earlier centuries is a choice to review.

No building is a ceremonial or sacred place. Iron tools came only from Europeans.

**Stores and hard years.** Storage pits and smokehouses were part of many towns in the region: food and goods put by for winter and hard years, security rather than profit (sources as above: Snow 1994 for the pits; drying and smoking to be sourced).

**The land and the fur trade.** Harvesting draws on the land; over-harvesting wears it down. Demand for beaver pelts emptied whole regions of beaver in the 17th century and pushed the trade inland; the competition for furs is often given as one cause of the mid-17th-century wars between the Haudenosaunee and their neighbours, though historians weigh it against others. Beaver were lost across whole regions, Europeans' land included (why the game measures the land's health per region). Tired garden soils, and towns moving every decade or two as soils and firewood ran out, are a later idea for the game.
Sources: William Cronon, *Changes in the Land: Indians, Colonists, and the Ecology of New England* (Hill and Wang, 1983); Harold A. Innis, *The Fur Trade in Canada* (1930; University of Toronto Press); on the wars, Daniel K. Richter, *The Ordeal of the Longhouse* (University of North Carolina Press, 1992).

**Goods and trade.** The peoples' goods: food, furs, hides, fish, maize, tools, clothing, canoes; trade goods (iron tools, cloth, kettles, muskets) came only from Europeans. Peoples took up iron tools, kettles, muskets and horses as they served them, and that is continuity too (T10, T14).

**Epidemics.** Epidemics after contact were the largest fact of contact for the peoples; historically they struck the peoples far harder than the colonists (T12; the game applies the same rules to both sides, Simon's decision). Sources: to be given (see [Open items](#9-open-items-to-be-sourced-or-checked)).

## 3. Standing, kin and leagues

(Note: T10, T14, section 4.)

**Gifts and standing.** Among many North-eastern peoples, gifts made and renewed alliances, and a leader's influence rested on what he gave rather than what he kept. The ceremonies that went with gifts and promises (condolence, wampum at councils) are not modelled, named as actions or shown as buttons.
Source: Richard White, *The Middle Ground: Indians, Empires, and Republics in the Great Lakes Region, 1650–1815* (Cambridge University Press, 1991).

**Kin ties** (deferred to a separate scenario, kept for it). Peoples joined others, as the Tuscarora joined the Haudenosaunee League in the 1720s as its sixth nation (Richter 1992); in the fur trade, marriages tied French and native communities.
Sources: Richter, *The Ordeal of the Longhouse* (1992); Susan Sleeper-Smith, *Indian Women and French Men: Rethinking Cultural Encounter in the Western Great Lakes* (University of Massachusetts Press, 2001).

**Leagues.** Peoples of the North-east joined in lasting leagues: the Haudenosaunee Confederacy (the Five Nations, later Six) settled common affairs in a council of its nations; the Wabanaki Confederacy joined peoples of today's Maine and the Maritimes from the later 17th century. Further peoples joined later, as the Tuscarora did. The founding accounts are the nations' own and are not used (T9).
Sources: Richter, *The Ordeal of the Longhouse* (1992); National Museum of the American Indian, *Haudenosaunee Guide for Educators* (Smithsonian, 2009); for the Wabanaki, Colin G. Calloway (ed.), *Dawnland Encounters: Indians and Europeans in Northern New England* (University Press of New England, 1991) and the Abbe Museum, Bar Harbor, Maine (a Wabanaki museum). The Wabanaki Confederacy's founding date: to be sourced.

**Diplomacy.** The Speakers (council, below) rest on the record of Iroquois diplomacy: Francis Jennings, William N. Fenton, Mary A. Druke and David R. Miller (eds.), *The History and Culture of Iroquois Diplomacy* (Syracuse University Press, 1985).

## 6. Events and terms

(Note: section 13 "The scenario".)

- **The fishing fleets on the Grand Banks** (the event's years 1540–1580): every summer the fishing fleets of Portugal, France, Spain and England crossed to the Grand Banks and the coasts of Newfoundland for cod, and dried their catch ashore; crews wintered and some stayed. Source: to be given (Barkham 1984 and Pope 1997 cover the 16th-century fisheries in part; to be checked).
- **The year without a summer** (1816): snow in June and frost in every month of the summer brought killing cold to New England and the St. Lawrence, after a great eruption far away (Tambora, 1815) darkened the sky. Source: to be given.
- Hard years and epidemics, the region's other hardships, are mechanics rather than events. No event makes light of the epidemics, the wars or the loss of land.
- **Terms:** the title screen's lines and tagline; no Norse, Greenland or Vikings anywhere (T17).
