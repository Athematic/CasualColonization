# The Silver Road

**Logline.** Young muleteer Kusi Mamani drives the Crown's silver down from the mountain of mines, saving every coin he earns to bring his father home from his turn of forced labour there. When a landslide cuts the road four days before the treasury's deadline, the only way through is the old high road and the grass rope bridge his village has kept for centuries, which this year has gone unrepaired because too many of its men are in the mines. To save the silver, a nervous Spanish officer has to learn to twist grass rope, cross a gorge with his eyes open, and decide what the law owes the people who keep the King's roads.

**Cover moment.** The mule train winds down the stone switchbacks above the green terraces: Kusi in his poncho and knitted cap leading a loaded mule, the llama Ch'aska carrying the village's bundle and staring at the viewer, Lieutenant Diego on his grey mule, and Uncle Wayra behind with the second mule, with the white town, the blue lake and the snow peaks below and a condor over the valley. It appears on **page 10**.

**Setting.** The high Andes of the Viceroyalty of Peru, on the mule road from the silver mountain of Potosí towards a lake town with a royal treasury office. Dry season, July 1741.

**Cast.**
- **Kusi Mamani** (17): Quechua muleteer. Red striped poncho, knitted cap with earflaps in red, yellow and blue, white shirt, dark trousers, sandals. Quick, cheerful, sings to his mules, and carries a small leather purse that he never spends from.
- **Ch'aska**: Kusi's llama (her name means "morning star"). Cream and brown, carrying a striped bundle of the village's woven cloth and chuño (freeze-dried potatoes) to trade. Very opinionated, and stares at people.
- **Wayra Quispe**: Kusi's uncle and master of the mule train. Red-and-brown poncho, knitted cap, a khipu (knotted cords) at his belt for keeping the train's accounts. Calm, few words, knows every stone of the road.
- **Lieutenant Diego de Arroyo**: officer escorting the Crown's silver. Cream coat, red waistcoat, dark blue cloak, broad black hat, short dark beard. Rides a grey mule called Princesa. Proper, honest, homesick for the flat plains of Castile, very afraid of heights.
- **Mama Asunta Quispe**: elder of Kusi's village and Wayra's older sister. Layered skirts, a dark shawl, a flat fringed hat, a spindle always in her hands. Sharp, funny, in charge.
- **Tata Simón Huamán**: the village's old bridge master. White-haired, wiry, a coil of grass rope over one shoulder.
- **Tomasa Mamani** (12): Kusi's sister. Red shawl, braids, tireless at twisting grass cord, and not at all scared of officers.
- **Don Baltasar Ugarte**: agent of a mine owner, with his own mule train of silver. Fine black coat, silver buttons, a voice like a creaking gate. Wants to arrive first and be thanked by everyone important.
- **Mateo Mamani**: Kusi's father, away serving his turn in the mines. Seen only at the end.

*(Quechua words used: allillanchu (hello, are you well?), sulpayki (thank you), apu (mountain lord), Pachamama (Mother Earth), apacheta (cairn at a pass), ayni (help given and returned), mink'a (shared work for the community), qhapaq ñan (the old royal road), q'eswa (grass rope), khipu, chuño. Check with a Quechua language consultant before lettering, as usage differs between regions. Personal names are invented.)*

## PAGE 1

1. Wide opening panel. Before dawn, high on the bare puna, frost on the ground, a line of mules being loaded beside a stone corral. Behind, far off, the great reddish cone of a mountain with a town at its foot, small lights in the dark.
   CAPTION: The silver mountain of Potosí, 1741. Silver from its mines went down to the treasuries in long trains of mules.
   CAPTION: And every year, under the Crown's draft called the mit'a, men from the villages were sent to work inside the mountain, by turns.

2. Close on Kusi in his knitted cap, tightening a strap on a mule loaded with leather sacks and a wooden chest, singing softly to it.
   KUSI (singing): ♪ Walk slow, my sister, walk slow, the road is long, the road is long... ♪

3. Close on a small leather purse that Kusi tucks deep inside his shirt.
   CAPTION: Kusi's father had been called to the mountain in January.
   CAPTION: The law let a village pay silver instead of a man. Kusi was saving it, coin by coin.

4. Lieutenant Diego, in his cream coat and broad hat, strides up importantly with a ledger, and comes face to face with Ch'aska the llama, who stares at him unblinkingly from very close.
   DIEGO: Good morning, er... animal.

5. Ch'aska looks at him for a long moment. Then she very deliberately spits on his boot.
   SFX: PTOO
   KUSI: Ah. She likes you. She only spits at people she likes.
   DIEGO: And the people she doesn't like?
   KUSI: She spits much higher.

## PAGE 2

1. In the grey dawn light, Uncle Wayra stands at the head of the train, running the knotted cords of a khipu through his fingers, counting the loads.
   WAYRA: Thirty-two mules. Sixty-four sacks. Eight chests. All here.

2. Diego, consulting his ledger, frowns.
   DIEGO: My ledger says thirty-three mules.

3. Wayra, without a word, walks him slowly down the whole line, touching each mule once. Diego counts along, lips moving. They reach the end.
   DIEGO: ...thirty-one, thirty-two.
   DIEGO: Ah. I counted my own mule.

4. Diego looks at the khipu with new respect.
   DIEGO: Those knots are better than my book.
   WAYRA: The knots don't get tired, and they don't count themselves.

5. Diego climbs onto his grey mule, Princesa, and addresses the train with great formality.
   DIEGO: This silver is the King's fifth, owed to the Crown. It must be in the treasury of the lake town before the convoy to the coast leaves on the Feast of Santiago. Nine days.
   KUSI (to Wayra, low): Nine days. It's always eight and a half by this road.
   WAYRA: Then we'd better not waste the half.

## PAGE 3

1. A dramatic vertical panel. The mule train on a narrow stone road cut into a cliff face, a sheer drop on one side to a river far below like a silver thread.

2. Diego, on Princesa, has his eyes screwed shut and his hands clamped on the saddle. Princesa walks calmly on the very edge.
   DIEGO: Is it far down?
   KUSI (walking beside him): Not very.
   DIEGO: How not very?
   KUSI: You'd have time to say a short prayer. Not a long one.

3. Diego, eyes still shut, talks to calm himself.
   DIEGO: Where I come from, near Burgos, it is flat. Wheat to the edge of the sky. You can see your mother coming from three days away.

4. The pass. A large cairn of stones on the top of the ridge, an apacheta, with snow peaks behind. Kusi and Wayra each place a stone on it and tuck three coca leaves under it.
   KUSI: For the apus, the mountain lords. And for Pachamama, so the road stays kind.

5. Diego, off his mule, hesitates. Then he picks up a stone, turns it over in his hand, and places it carefully on the cairn.
   DIEGO: For my mother in Burgos. She worries.
   WAYRA (nodding approvingly): Then the mountain will know there is someone waiting for you.

## PAGE 4

1. The far side of the pass, day four. The train comes round a bend and stops abruptly. The front mules balk and stamp.
   SFX: HRRRNK!

2. Wide panel. Ahead, the whole road has vanished. An unseasonal storm in the night has brought down a landslide of mud and boulders; a great raw brown scar runs down the mountain where the road used to be.
   CAPTION: In the night, out of season, the rain had come, and taken half the mountainside with it.

3. Diego, pale, unrolls a map.
   DIEGO: The valley road goes round. How long?
   WAYRA: Ten days.
   DIEGO: We have five. If we miss the convoy, the King's silver sits in a cellar for a year, and I...
   DIEGO (quietly): ...I go home to Burgos in disgrace.

4. A clatter of hooves behind them. A second mule train arrives, led by a man in a fine black coat with silver buttons, riding a handsome black mule: Don Baltasar Ugarte.
   UGARTE: Well, well. Arroyo's train, stuck in the mud. How sad.

5. Close on Ugarte, smirking.
   UGARTE: I shall take the valley road, and I shall push my drivers day and night. And when I arrive, I shall tell the Treasurer that YOU lost the King's fifth on a mountainside.

6. Ugarte's train turns back down towards the valley. His own Quechua drivers, at the back, exchange a long-suffering look with Wayra and Kusi.
   UGARTE'S DRIVER (quietly, passing): Day and night, he says. He'll ride. We'll walk.

## PAGE 5

1. Kusi climbs a rock beside the slide and looks up the mountainside. High above, a thin line of old stone road runs along the slope: worn, grassy, but clearly built.
   KUSI: Uncle. The qhapaq ñan.

2. Diego joins him, following his pointing arm.
   DIEGO: That old path?
   KUSI: The old royal road, from before the Spaniards came. Steep, but straight. It crosses the gorge at my village. Two days to the lake town from there.

3. Wayra, unusually, looks worried.
   WAYRA: The road is good. It is the bridge.

4. Close on Wayra, explaining gravely.
   WAYRA: Our village keeps the rope bridge over the gorge. We have woven it new every year since our grandparents' grandparents. Grass rope only lasts a year.
   WAYRA: This year we couldn't. Too many of our men are in the mountain. The old bridge is sagging. It won't carry mules.

5. Diego looks down at his hands, then up at the two muleteers.
   DIEGO: How many hands does it take to make a new one?
   WAYRA: Every hand we have, and more.
   DIEGO: Then I am two more.

6. Kusi stares at the officer, surprised. Ch'aska, beside Diego, sniffs his hands suspiciously.
   KUSI: You?
   DIEGO: I can't climb a cliff with my eyes open. But I can certainly work.

## PAGE 6

1. Wide panel. Kusi's village on a green shelf high above the gorge: round stone houses with thatched roofs, a little adobe chapel, terraces of potatoes. The mule train winds in along the old road. Children and dogs run out to meet them.

2. Mama Asunta, spindle in hand, stands in the village square, inspecting Kusi with mock severity.
   KUSI: Allillanchu, Mama Asunta!
   MAMA ASUNTA: Allillanmi. You're thin. And you've brought an officer. Is he hungry too?
   DIEGO (removing his hat): A little, señora.

3. Kusi's sister Tomasa runs up and throws her arms round Kusi, then looks Diego up and down.
   TOMASA: Is he here to count us?
   DIEGO: No. I'm here to ask for help.
   TOMASA: Then he's a strange officer.

4. Old Tata Simón, the bridge master, a coil of grass rope over his shoulder, listens to Wayra and nods slowly, gazing at the sky.
   TATA SIMÓN: Three days of work, with fewer hands than we need. In two days? Only if everyone comes.

5. Mama Asunta climbs up on the stone edge of the village well and calls out across the square.
   MAMA ASUNTA: Mink'a! Tomorrow, the bridge! Everyone who can twist, twist! Everyone who can carry, carry!
   MAMA ASUNTA: And our guests, the muleteers and the officer... they twist too.

## PAGE 7

1. Next morning, a wide panel full of people: the whole village, plus every muleteer from the train, sitting on the hillside in rows with great heaps of long, golden ichu grass, twisting it into thin cords. The mules graze below.
   CAPTION: In the Andes, a bridge is made of grass, of hands, and of everyone.

2. Diego sits cross-legged among them, cloak off, sleeves rolled, trying to twist a cord. It comes out lumpy and immediately unravels. The children around him giggle.
   DIEGO: Why does it hate me?

3. Tomasa plonks down beside him and shows him: two strands of wet grass rolled against the thigh in one direction, then twisted together in the other.
   TOMASA: One way, then the other way. They hold each other. Like people.

4. Diego tries again, slowly. This time a neat, firm cord forms. He holds it up, delighted, like a prize.
   DIEGO: Look! It holds!
   TOMASA: Good. Now another thousand.

5. Small panel. Ch'aska, unseen behind them, is happily eating Diego's heap of grass.
   SFX: MUNCH MUNCH

6. Mama Asunta, passing with her spindle, gently scolds Ch'aska away and adds a little pile of new grass to Diego's heap.
   MAMA ASUNTA: Today you help us. Tomorrow we help you carry your King's silver across. That is ayni.
   DIEGO: In Castile we would call that a contract.
   MAMA ASUNTA: Here we call it being a neighbour.

## PAGE 8

1. Afternoon at the gorge. The thin cords have been braided into thick ropes and those into great cables as thick as a man's leg. Teams of villagers and muleteers haul them across to the far side on lines; Tata Simón directs from a stone platform at the bridge head.
   TATA SIMÓN: Pull! Together! Now hold!

2. Diego, pulling on a cable in a line between Wayra and a village woman, notices something carved into the old stone platform: a worn cross and a weathered date. He frowns, remembering.
   DIEGO: This bridge has carried the royal road for two hundred years?
   WAYRA: Longer. Before your kings, for the Inka's road. After, for yours.

3. Close on Diego, a thought striking him.
   DIEGO (thought): The old ordinances... Villages that keep the King's bridges and roads serve the Crown there. Their work is counted as their service.
   DIEGO (thought): Instead of sending their men to the mines.

4. He turns to Wayra, urgently.
   DIEGO: Wayra. Does anyone in the treasury know your village keeps this bridge?
   WAYRA (shrugging): The bridge has always been here. Nobody in the city ever asked who keeps it up.
   DIEGO: Then somebody is going to tell them.

5. As the sun sets, the last cables are tied off at both ends. The great new bridge hangs across the gorge, golden in the evening light, side railings woven to the floor.
   CAPTION: By dusk on the second day, the bridge was new.

## PAGE 9

1. Morning. The crossing. Wide panel: the new rope bridge swaying over the depths, the river roaring far below. Kusi leads the first mule out onto it, talking softly to it. The bridge holds.
   SFX (far below): RRROOOOOOAR

2. One by one the mules cross, each led by a villager or muleteer, the silver chests swaying on their backs. Ch'aska strolls across on her own, unbothered, as though going to market.

3. Diego stands at the bridge head with Princesa's lead rope in his hand, frozen, staring down.
   DIEGO: I can't.

4. Kusi comes back across the bridge and takes the other side of Princesa's halter, standing beside Diego.
   KUSI: Don't look down. Look at Ch'aska. She's waiting for you on the other side. If you don't come, she'll be very offended.

5. Halfway across, Diego walks slowly, one hand on the grass railing, his eyes OPEN, fixed on the far side, Kusi beside him. Below, the gorge. Above, a condor wheels.
   DIEGO: I'm doing it. My eyes are open. My mother would faint.
   KUSI: Keep walking. You can faint later.

6. On the far side, Diego steps off the bridge onto solid stone. Ch'aska walks straight up to him and, very gently, rests her chin on his shoulder.
   KUSI: There. Now she likes you properly.

## PAGE 10

*(Cover moment.)*

1. LARGE PANEL, two-thirds of the page, matching the cover. Two days later, the narrow stone road zigzags down a steep green mountainside. In the foreground, Ch'aska the llama, carrying the striped bundle of village cloth and chuño, looks straight out at the viewer. Behind her Kusi, in red poncho and knitted cap, leads a brown mule loaded with leather sacks and wooden chests. Beside him rides Diego on Princesa, in his cream coat, red waistcoat, dark cloak and broad black hat, upright and looking at the view, not at the drop. Further up the road, Uncle Wayra leads the next loaded mule. Below the road, stepped green terraces curve round the slope; far below, a white town with red tile roofs and a church tower beside a deep blue lake; beyond it, snow-capped peaks and a few clean white clouds. A condor glides above the valley.
   CAPTION: Two days later, with a day to spare.
   DIEGO: Look at that. You can see a whole country from here.
   KUSI: You're looking down, Teniente.
   DIEGO: ...So I am.

2. Close on Diego, a little shaky but proud, laughing at himself.
   DIEGO: Don't tell my mother. She'll say I've turned into a mountain goat, and then she'll come and visit.

3. Small panel, far below on the valley floor, tiny in the distance: Ugarte's mule train, still crawling round the far end of the lake.
   WAYRA (pointing): Day and night, he said.
   KUSI: The night part went well, at least. They're all asleep.

## PAGE 11

1. Inside the royal treasury office in the lake town: thick white walls, a heavy iron-bound strongbox, a long table, a crucifix. The Treasurer, a plump man in a black coat, checks off the silver chests as muleteers bring them in. Diego stands at attention. Wayra stands beside him with his khipu.
   TREASURER: All eight chests. And sixty-four sacks, by the muleteer's count and yours?
   DIEGO: By his count, señor. His is better.

2. The Treasurer stamps the receipt. Diego, instead of leaving, places a folded letter on the table with a firm hand.
   DIEGO: One more thing. My report on the journey.
   DIEGO: The village above the gorge keeps the King's bridge on the royal road, as the old ordinances require, and saved the King's fifth this week by doing so. Their work is service to the Crown.

3. The Treasurer reads, purses his lips, and looks up.
   TREASURER: You are asking for their men to be counted as bridge keepers... and not called to the mountain.
   DIEGO: I am asking for the law to be kept, señor. All of it, not just the parts that bring silver in.

4. A clatter at the door. Ugarte bursts in, dusty and furious, his coat buttons undone.
   UGARTE: Treasurer! I must report that Arroyo has lost the King's silver in a landslide—

5. Ugarte stops. The eight chests are stacked neatly beside the strongbox. Diego, Wayra and Kusi turn to look at him politely.
   TREASURER (dry): Has he? Then whose are these?

6. Small panel. Ugarte, opening and closing his mouth like a fish. Behind him, through the door, Ch'aska stares at him with narrowed eyes, preparing to spit very high.

## PAGE 12

1. The village square, two months later. Kusi pours coins from his little leather purse into a clay bowl on Mama Asunta's table. Other villagers add coins and small silver pieces from trading their cloth and chuño in the lake town. Diego, in his cream coat, sets down a sealed paper beside the bowl.
   CAPTION: The answer came from the city two months later.

2. Mama Asunta reads the paper aloud to the gathered village, her voice steady, Tomasa at her elbow.
   MAMA ASUNTA: "The keepers of the bridge on the royal road are to be counted as serving the Crown in that work." From next year, our men keep the bridge. They are not to be called to the mountain.
   CAPTION: It would not bring everyone home from the mountain. But it would bring this village's men home.

3. Kusi looks at the bowl of coins, then at Mama Asunta.
   KUSI: And this? My father's turn isn't over until January.
   MAMA ASUNTA: It is now. The village pays for its own. That is also ayni.

4. The far end of the rope bridge over the gorge. A figure walks across it towards the village: Mateo Mamani, Kusi's father, thin and tired, in a worn poncho, a bundle on his back, but walking steadily. Kusi and Tomasa run to meet him at the bridge head.
   TOMASA: PAPA!
   KUSI (quietly, his face full): Papa.

5. Close: Mateo holds his son and daughter together, his eyes closed, at the end of the golden grass bridge. Wayra and Mama Asunta stand a little way off. Diego, beside them, takes off his hat.
   MATEO: You came by the old road, I heard. And brought an officer across the bridge.
   KUSI: He twisted the rope himself. Badly at first.

6. FINAL PANEL, wide. The whole village, the muleteers and Diego gather at the bridge head under the snow peaks, the new grass bridge golden across the gorge, a condor wheeling high above. In the foreground, exactly as on the cover, Ch'aska the llama looks straight at the reader, chewing thoughtfully, with Diego's broad black hat sitting crookedly on her head.
   CAPTION: Ayni. Today you help us. Tomorrow we help you.
   DIEGO (off, faintly): ...Has anyone seen my hat?
